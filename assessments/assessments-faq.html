Summative Portfolio

Read this FAQ alongside the summative portfolio brief, which sets out the requirements and suggested structure for each component in more detail.

What is meant by an “analysis focus”?

Your analysis focus is an issue, problem, intellectual puzzle, or area of enquiry that gives a focus to your analysis and brings your portfolio together. You can think of it as a main research question, but it does not have to take that form. The term was chosen deliberately to provide flexibility. The focus might be a particular issue, an specific area of research, exploration of data to assess its value to a topic, or initial analysis that helps identify and refine questions for further research.

Each analysis findings component should address its own more specific analysis question (or questions). Both analysis findings components should contribute in some way to the overall focus, but they can explore different aspects / dimensions of it. Given the word counts and time for working on the analysis, neither component is expected to fully answer the analysis questions nor provide a full answer to the analysis focus. Neither do the analysis findings components need to directly feed into the other.

What is important is the ‘alignment’ of your analysis design that threads it together. Use the analysis focus and analysis questions to explain your choices of data, data management / wrangling, sample (and its representativeness), and analysis approaches.

Which analysis approaches can I use?

Any valid social science analysis approach covered on the programme or beyond it are permitted. Across Components 2 and 3, you must though include both qualitative and quantitative approaches. You can have one component of each, combine a qualitative or quantitative component with a mixed-methods component, or use mixed methods for both. If both components use mixed methods, they can use the same combination of methods - as you will still sufficiently demonstrate range in using mixed methods across both.

The exception is students specifically asked by SGSSS to concentrate on qualitative or quantitative approaches. Both components may then be from the same broad method, but should demonstrate meaningful differences in analytical approach. For qualitative analysis, examples of valid options include thematic, discourse, narrative, phenomenological, framework, and content analysis. Quantitative options include both exploratory and inferential approaches. So, any statistical approach which aligns with, is appropriate for, and provides findings relevant to your analysis question(s) is fine to use.

For SGSSS students who are required to do qualitative analysis for both components, it may be the case that both approaches you plan to do fall within what could broadly be called thematic analysis. What would be critical here is ensuring there analyses remain sufficiently different. It is an unfortunate situation that a lot of published research refers vaguely to having done a thematic analysis without much more specifics than that. An experiential thematic analysis of people’s lived experiences could differ from a thematic analysis of policymakers’ accounts of the policy implementation processes.

Across all forms of analysis, be specific about the analysis being undertaken and how it aligns with the overall analysis design. Choose approaches that suit your questions and data, considering whether there is any more specific approach which best suits your analysis plans. For example, ‘critical discourse analysis’ as a more specific approach which might be better suited to your analysis question(s) than ‘discourse analysis’.

Can I use the same data for both findings components?

Yes, but ensure this makes sense for your questions and approaches. Using different datasets is also perfectly fine if they help answer / contribute towards different aspects of your analysis focus.

There needs to be alignment across your components and the choice of analysis approach justified. Indeed, consider and explain what each analysis contributes in terms of the analysis question(s) for the component and the overall analysis focus.

Applying thematic analysis and then framework analysis to exactly the same two interviews would not, on its own, establish a reason for two components. Instead, what would be more appropriate would be discourse analysis of policy documents and phenomenological analysis of interview transcripts. Here there is a clearer rationale behind the two approaches and the data they are used alongside.

Another example where it would be easier to use the same data and there is clearer justified rationale for that would be undertaking quantitative text analysis of 100 newspaper articles and qualitative analysis of a selection of four of those articles.

Whilst it is not required for one component to feed into another, this combination would also be well suited for the quantitative analysis helping inform the sampling for the qualitative analysis. However, to be clear, the quantitative analysis should answer its own substantive question and not just inform the sampling for the qualitative analysis. Its findings might also help you select material for qualitative analysis, but producing that sample is not its sole purpose.

What data can I use? What about ethics?

You may use any data (in the end all data is ‘messy’ in some way, and it will be digital in some form - even if it is a transcript of an in-person interview, that is a digital record of the original interaction) that does not require ethical approval for your use in this assessment.

Options include data archives, data portals, data made publicly available by governments and companies, newspaper articles, web data, data available through APIs, and public documents - like parliamentary transcripts, policy documents, etc. If you are considering data or approaches beyond those covered on the course and unsure about them, please get in touch!

To avoid ethical issues which would require an ethics application with social media data, use an existing anonymised or pseudonymised dataset from a credible source, in which the original account identifiers have been removed or replaced. You must not collect social-media posts or anything similar such as newspaper comments directly for this assessment. An original account name being a pseudonym - such as commonly used on Reddit instead of real names - does not make its contributions anonymised data as would be understood by university ethics policies.

However, Wikipedia article content, Talk pages, project pages, etc and edit histories of these can be analysed, including editors’ contributions to producing and revising pages. For example, you could examine what proportion of a page different editors have contributed (though still good practice to anonymise all account names). What you cannot analyse though is any editors’ personal pages and personal information. It is possible on Wikipedia for editors to create their own page with further info about themselves, do not include any such page in your analysis - that would move to using data which brings in similar ethical considerations as social media data / comments.

Can I use the same data covered in the labs?

Yes. You can use the same data sources as we covered in the labs. I tried to incorporate a diversity of examples to show range of things that are possible with R and populate the pages with examples of working code with different data sources which can modify based on your analysis plans.

So to clarify: One of the example page’s default selection should not become your sample because the code in the example produced it. Consider what you would need to modify in the code to collect and prepare the data which aligns with your analysis plans.

For example, modify the code we used for the Holyrood JSON files - or even include other Scottish Parliament JSON files - to help identify debates relevant to your own analysis question(s). In the example, we looked at stage 3 votes in 2025 and classified them by whether there was an overwhelming majority in support or not. Instead, you might be interested in comparing debates where Greens supported the SNP government’s legislation verses ones where they voted against the legislation. Alternatively, collecting transcripts discussing a particular issue over time; finding all transcripts for a specific committee; and so on.

With the Guardian API, much of the code can stay the same while you change the search, types of article, date range, keywords, or similar for the query of articles to return. Even parakeets could be a suitable topic if your question and selection justify it, though even there based on your specific question there could still be aspects that would change. For instance, if only interested in Editorials and Comment pieces and not regular news reporting. There is no requirement to invent an entirely different topic - its about how to modify what we covered based on your analysis plans.

For all data also consider its social production - who produced it, for what purposes, how it was collected or constructed, and what does it record or represent. Explain why it is suitable for your question and what limitations its original purpose or coverage creates. This is particularly important when reusing data collected for a different purpose to its original production.

For any secondary / archival dataset that already exists as a file to download, such considerations are important - especially considering the original research questions behind the original data collection and the suitability of the data in relation to your own analysis plans.

Whilst you do not need to go through the same steps as with working with an API, still consider important things like the selection of variables, any cleaning; recording; creation of derived variables; choosing whether to use a subset of data and/or link datasets; and so on.

With qualitative analysis, select the subset of data that is most relevant to your analysis plans. Secondary analysis of qualitative datasets - especially large qualitative datasets - do not often re-analysis all data in the archive. What is instead important is rationale for choosing the dataset in general and the specific subset of data analysed.

A dataset does not have to be a perfect fit. You can acknowledge its limitations and consider the implications for in what way it can be used for your question and how you interpret the findings. For example, a variable may be an imperfect measure where want to avoid overstating the interpretation of any stats calculated using it. With qualitative research, the data may be strong on one area but not on another as that aspect was not important for the team who did the original interviews.

How much data should I analyse?

The amount should be appropriate to the type of analysis and time frame of the course. You are not expected to analyse the quantity of qualitative data that a full research project might require, for example.

As a rough guide, for a qualitative component, text comparable to two interviews lasting 45-60 minutes each can be sufficient. Pending on your analysis question(s) though you may want to analyse more - e.g. if comparing three groups, you’d want at least an interview representative of each group.

However, it is not possible to give a precise item count. Newspaper articles, policy documents, social-media posts, web pages, interviews, and focus groups, all vary considerably in length and richness. Analysis approaches which require a detailed line-by-line analysis also has different time demands compared to a broader framework analysis that aims more at breadth.

For quantitative analysis is should mostly remain possible to use larger datasets except where any data cleaning or preparation would require a lot of manual work that cannot be handled easily by R. That should be relatively rare though. Whilst working with some APIs can take some time figuring them out, once you figure it out it can quickly scale to level of data that you need. What again is more important is relevance of the data. Having 100 items directly relevant to your analysis question is better than having 10,000 items with lot of irrelevant data.

As always though, please get in touch if you are unsure what amount is appropriate for your proposed analysis.

Do I have to use R?

R is strongly encouraged, but it is not required.

Manual collection using websites like Nexis and PressReader, official and third-party tools which provide graphical interface to find and/or work with data, and using website’s own search and navigation options are OK to use - as long as this can be justified in relation to your analysis design.

For example, if your analysis focus is around a specific social policy and one component is a discourse analysis of the policy documents related to it, then manually downloading the documents for analysis is perfectly fine.

Whichever tools you use, explain how you selected, collected, organised, and prepared the data. Importantly, consider any useful metadata, such as publication dates, authorship, document type, or length. Even in qualitative research metadata can be useful for making sampling decisions, making comparisons, additional context, etc.

Indeed, whilst R is sadly often thought of solely as a tool for quantitative methods, as I tried to cover across the lab examples, there is vast - and largely untapped - potential for using R to support qualitative work in myriad ways, such as for identifying a sample as well as collecting and preparing metadata and texts for analysis. R similarly can be useful for preparing figures for your methods or additional information in your analysis sections.

Importantly too is that across the labs and the examples used in each lab, we have generally increased the complexity of the data we are working with. However, there is absolutely no requirement to use data which requires a lot more code to work with. I included diversity of examples to show ways to handle common problems that you may encounter in practice.

Do I need to create a multiple linear regression model for a quantitative component?

No, there is no requirement to do linear regression - though we again strongly encourage used of correlation and regression where they are appropriate. A well-developed exploratory analysis though can be completely acceptable.

What is of most importance with “exploratory analysis” is that the analyse is driven by something - such as questions and curiosity about patterns, comparisons, trends, and/or relationships. Your analysis should not be a simple reporting of what the data looks like in the abstract, but explore the data based on your analysis question(s).

In other words, within the word count available, give most attention to what helps understand the data in relation to your question(s) and findings which answer the question. Say your question concerns changing trends across two groups. When discussing your sample, it could be appropriate to include a table of descriptive statistics of the key variables of interest for each group and the total sample (if that fits neatly into a single table). Then within the findings, focus on the figures and stats which have relevance to answering your analysis question(s).

Interpret what the patterns mean in context, including relevant limitations. The aim is to help the reader understand what you have learnt about the issue through exploring the data. (Note: whilst in the labs we have included steps to visualise the data as we step through preparing it, within our analysis we would not include things like how two datasets looked before joining them.)

What if the data turns out to not be as useful as first thought?

Do not worry if you selected data with intent to do multiple linear regression and then after finding datasets, linking them together, and doing further cleaning, you then discover that the key variables of interest do not meet the assumptions for doing linear regression. Or, similarly, that your model has no significant results or results which are statistical significant but with small coefficients without practical significance.

These are all perfectly valid things to uncover when working with data. If data turns out not not to meet the assumptions for linear regression then you can include the figures to evidence it, explore any potential reasons behind why, and any exploratory analysis relevant to answering your analysis question(s). A plot might reveal an interesting pattern even where the data ends up not supporting the model you intended to use.

Ensure though to keep your claims within what the analyses you have conducted can support. Include recognition of what your analysis can investigate and what remains unanswered from your original analysis question(s).

An aim of this module is to give you space to develop your analysis skills in the direction of your choosing. So another option could be using alternative regression techniques more suited to the data. However, as these are not covered on the programme so far, they are not a requirement to do - just highlighting these an option too.

Which figures and/or text excerpts should I include?

There are two critical considerations to make: (1) what evidence best helps a reader understand your sample and support your most relevant findings and reflections (2) which of these does that best within the space available.

Every figure or text excerpt should have a clear purpose and be adequately discussed in the main text. Be selective with appendices too. Adding more material does not help if there is insufficient room to explain its relevance. Deciding what is the most important evidence is part of the work involved in doing analysis.

For qualitative analysis, use selected excerpts (i.e. quotations from the texts you analysed) to develop and substantiate your interpretation. When discussing methods, it can be useful to include a table of final codes used, but use the space available for findings to build and advance an interpretation rather than descriptively summarising each code in turn.

With quantitative analysis, you do not need to display every output produced while exploring or preparing data. The lab examples include code to view the data at intermediate stages for the purposes of helping understand what the code is doing. What is more important is formatted tables and plots that contribute towards an understanding of the sample and answering the analysis question(s).

What counts towards the word count?

The following are not included in the word count:

  • All figures (tables and plots), including their captions.
  • Text excerpts quoted from the data you are analysing.
  • Your bibliography.
  • Any appendices.

Whilst quotations from academic literature normally do count towards the word count, there is an exception if you are analysing academic texts - such as a discourse analysis of journal articles. In that case, text excerpts from them to evidence your analysis do not count towards the word count. All quotations from other academic texts do as per usual.

What files should I submit?

Please submit one combined Word document or HTML file where possible that contains all three components. It is possible to submit more than one file though if needed, but keeping the portfolio together as a single file is preferable.

I’d encourage you to try using R Markdown to produce an HTML file - but, again, this is not a requirement of the assessment. It is perfectly fine to copy any of your tables and plots over to Word and submit your portfolio as a Word document.

You do not need to submit any code nor copies of the data analysed. (If using R Markdown to write-up your analysis, you can set ‘echo = false’ to hide the code but keep its output. Where do not want the output either being in your knitted HTML file, then use ‘include = false’.)

Where can I find further resources?

There are a plethora of useful open-access resources for R. Two good starting points:

  • R for Data Science: is a great Tidyverse based open access book that covers importing, tidying, transforming, exploring, joining, and visualising data. It is not a book you read front to back, but instead skim through to find examples relevant to what you are doing.
  • The Big Book of R: a searchable directory of open access R books organised by topic with ones covering APIs, web scraping, extracting elements from HTML, text analysis, network analysis, regression, ggplot, R Markdown, and more!!!

Most texts do assume a certain degree of prior knowledge and thus are slightly terse in explaining the code in their examples. The reference pages though aim to cover a lot of the basics of R, RStudio, and R Markdown that most texts will assume at some familiarity with.

Having difficulties with R?

Please do not suffer in silence! If an explanation remains difficult to follow, you cannot work out how to adapt an example, or you are unable to find examples for what you are wanting to do, please get in touch!

In your email, explain what you are trying to achieve and where you are encountering any issues. For coding problems, please include the relevant code and any error message in full.

I will aim to continue adding further examples on these pages - whether that is variants for working with data already covered or how to work with data not covered in the current pages.

Importantly, not only are we ‘here to help’ as staff - hearing from students where they are having difficulties is helpful for us to know what we need to add or improve in our teaching materials.

What GenAI use is permitted?

For the portfolio assessment, you may use genAI to seek textbook-style code examples, help understanding coding errors, or receive general guidance about analysis approaches and interpreting findings.

Do not use genAI to generate code that you can then copy/paste directly for the assessment. Similarly, do not use genAI to interpret your own specific analysis results. The work of analysing and interpreting must remain your own.

For general programme guidance about genAI use, including how to acknowledge any genAI use in your assessments, see Programme Moodle guidance on genAI.
